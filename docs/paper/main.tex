\begin{abstract}
Station-level forecasts of fine particulate matter (PM$_{2.5}$) one to two days ahead are needed for public health
advisories in Indian cities, yet learned forecasting models are rarely compared with an operational chemistry-transport
forecast under inputs that would actually be available at issue time. We present a leakage-safe benchmark over a fixed
panel of 116 Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) stations in 28 Indian cities, in which every input respects its
real publication latency and the test years are opened once, after the model is frozen. On this benchmark we evaluate
MW-AeroFusion-Net, a five-component model that combines causal wavelet bands, a wind-aware adaptive diffusion graph over
stations and upwind source regions, a ConvLSTM over gridded meteorology, fire and CAMS fields, a Transformer temporal
encoder, and source-group attribution gating. We compare it with persistence, climatology, raw and bias-corrected CAMS
global forecasts, LightGBM and five neural baselines at +1, +24 and +48\,h, in a past-only and a CAMS-informed setting.

\section{Introduction}
Fine particulate matter (PM$_{2.5}$) is the dominant air-quality risk in Indian cities. Winter inversions, crop-residue
burning in Punjab and Haryana, festival fireworks and pre-monsoon dust produce episodes in which concentrations exceed
the national standard many times over. Operational forecasts in India come from chemistry-transport systems such as
SAFAR~\cite{Beig_2021} and the IITM early-warning system~\cite{Ghude_2020,Jena_2021}, and from global products such as
the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS) forecasts. In parallel, learned spatio-temporal models for PM$_{2.5}$
are well established~\cite{Wang_2020,Liang_2023,Yi_2018,Zheng_2015}, including graph models for Indian
stations~\cite{Ejurothu_2022,Mandal_2023}, and wavelet or decomposition hybrids are
common~\cite{Bai_2016,Qiao_2019,Wang_2024,He_2025}.

Two questions are rarely answered in this literature. First, does a learned model beat a calibrated CAMS forecast at
matched lead times when its inputs are restricted to what was published at issue time? Many studies feed reanalysis
meteorology for future hours~\cite{Wang_2020}, normalise or decompose the whole series before
splitting~\cite{Quilty_2018}, or compare only against other neural networks. Second, which parts of a composite
architecture actually help? Multi-component models are usually reported as a whole, so the contribution of each part is
unknown.

We address both questions with a benchmark and a component analysis. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A \emph{leakage-safe protocol} for station-level PM$_{2.5}$ forecasting over India: a fixed station panel,
publication latencies for every input source, causal feature construction, training-only normalisation, and a test
split that is read once by a single script after model freeze.
\item \emph{MW-AeroFusion-Net}, which combines causal wavelet bands, a wind-aware adaptive diffusion graph, gridded
ConvLSTM context, a Transformer encoder and source-group gating, built rung by rung so that each component's effect
is measured, with leave-one-out ablations over five seeds.
\item A comparison against \emph{CAMS global forecasts} at matched leads, raw and with per-station bias correction,
together with LightGBM and five neural baselines, in a past-only setting and a CAMS-informed (post-processing) setting.
\item A \emph{validation of the learned attribution} produced by the gating layer, using deletion tests, weight
randomisation, seed stability and physical plausibility, reported whether each check passes or fails.
\end{enumerate}
Code, configurations, the station panel and the split hashes are released.\footnote{\blank{public repository URL}}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Spatio-temporal graph models.} Diffusion convolution~\cite{li2017diffusion} and learned adaptive
adjacency~\cite{Wu_2019} originated in traffic forecasting alongside spectral graph models~\cite{Yu_2018,kipf2016semisupervised}.
PM2.5-GNN~\cite{Wang_2020} adds distance and wind-direction edges for PM$_{2.5}$ over Chinese cities, but its offline
experiments feed reanalysis weather for future steps, which corresponds to our perfect-prognosis upper bound rather than an
operational forecast. Attention-based models~\cite{Liang_2018,Liang_2023,Yi_2018} and graph transformers~\cite{Zhang_2022,Lu_2025}
report strong results on Chinese networks. For India, cluster-based and spatially attentive graph networks have been
applied to CPCB stations~\cite{Ejurothu_2022,Mandal_2023}.

\textbf{Wavelet and decomposition hybrids.} Wavelet or mode decompositions followed by neural regressors are
common~\cite{Bai_2016,Qiao_2019,Wang_2024,He_2025,Lu_2025b}. Decomposing the full series before splitting leaks future
samples into the inputs~\cite{Quilty_2018}; boundary-corrected transforms avoid this~\cite{Barzegar_2021}. Several of the
studies we checked do not state when the decomposition is applied relative to the split. We compute the transform causally
inside each input window.

\textbf{CAMS evaluation and post-processing.} CAMS forecasts have been validated and calibrated with machine learning over
China and the United States~\cite{Wu_2020} and post-processed with model output statistics in Europe~\cite{Petetin_2022}.
Over the Indo-Gangetic Plain, CAMS and its reanalysis~\cite{Inness_2019} have been evaluated for aerosol composition and
optical depth~\cite{Soni_2021,Ansari_2023,Amarillo_2024}. In our search we did not find a study that scores CAMS global
PM$_{2.5}$ forecasts against CPCB stations at matched leads.

\textbf{Interpretability.} Variable-selection gating follows the Temporal Fusion Transformer~\cite{Lim_2021}. Attention
weights are not explanations by default~\cite{jain2019attention}, and saliency methods can fail randomisation
tests~\cite{adebayo2018sanity}; we therefore test the gate weights with deletion, Integrated
Gradients~\cite{sundararajan2017axiomatic} and randomisation, and compare them with TreeSHAP~\cite{lundberg2017a} on LightGBM.

\section{Data}
\textbf{Targets.} Hourly CPCB PM$_{2.5}$ from the XKDR bulk export of the CPCB continuous monitoring network (CC BY 4.0).
CPCB labels hours in Indian Standard Time at the start of the averaging interval; we convert them to UTC hours at ingest.
Station counts in Indian cities grew several-fold over the study period, so city medians drift. We therefore fix a panel
of 116 stations within 25\,km of 28 of the 33 study cities: stations reporting since 2020-01-01 with at least 60\% hourly
completeness through 2024. The five cities without such a station stay in the graph as unlabelled nodes. Quality
control removes sentinel and out-of-range values, flat-lines of six hours or more, and isolated spikes found with a
trailing 24\,h Hampel rule; a spike seen at two or more stations of the same city in the same hour is kept as a real
event. Targets are never filled; missing targets are masked in the loss. Table~\ref{tab:data} summarises the data card.

\textbf{Inputs.} (i) Pollutant history (PM$_{2.5}$, PM$_{10}$, NO$_2$, CO, O$_3$) with missing-value masks; gaps of up to
three hours are filled forward, never interpolated. (ii) ERA5 meteorology~\cite{Hersbach_2020} at each node: temperature,
humidity, 10\,m wind, pressure, precipitation, boundary-layer height and ventilation (wind speed $\times$ boundary-layer
height). (iii) VIIRS and MODIS active fires from NASA FIRMS: counts and fire radiative power (FRP) within 300\,km and
upwind counts within 500\,km. (iv) CAMS global forecasts of PM$_{2.5}$, total and dust aerosol optical depth. (v)
Calendar and event flags: hour and day of year, weekday, public holidays, a Diwali window, the stubble-burning season and
the 2020 lockdown. A gridded dataset on a 0.75$^\circ$ India grid ($44\times43$ cells, 3-hourly) holds 10\,m wind,
boundary-layer height, temperature, rasterised FRP and CAMS fields for the ConvLSTM branch.

\section{Method}
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the model. Let $N$ be the number of nodes (stations, unlabelled cities and upwind regions), $T=72$
the input window and $L=48$ the horizon. The input at issue time $t_0$ is $X\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times N\times F}$ with mask
$M$; the output is $\hat{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times N}$, the standardised $\log(1+\text{PM}_{2.5})$ at every lead.

\textbf{Causal wavelet bands.} For PM$_{2.5}$ and PM$_{10}$ we compute an undecimated \`a-trous db4 transform with three
levels inside each input window, using left-padded causal filters so that the band value at hour $t$ depends only on samples
at or before $t$. This avoids the leakage of full-series transforms~\cite{Quilty_2018}. The detail and approximation bands
are appended as input channels.

\textbf{Source-group attribution gating.} Input channels are partitioned into seven groups: pollutant history, traffic
proxy (NO$_2$, CO, weekday, holiday), meteorology, fire, CAMS, dust and calendar. For node $n$, a summary $s_{n,k}$ of group
$k$ (window mean and last value) gives gate weights
\begin{equation}
g_{n} = \operatorname{softmax}\big(W\,[\phi_1(s_{n,1});\dots;\phi_G(s_{n,G})]\big),
\end{equation}
and the per-step input is $h_{t,n}=\sum_k g_{n,k}\,P_k(x_{t,n,\mathcal{G}_k})$ with a linear projection $P_k$ per group,
in the spirit of TFT variable selection~\cite{Lim_2021}. An entropy penalty $\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[-\sum_k g_{n,k}\log g_{n,k}]$
encourages sparse weights (an L1 penalty would be constant, since the weights sum to one). We call $g_n$ a \emph{learned
attribution}; it is not a source apportionment.

\textbf{Temporal encoder.} A two-layer Transformer encoder~\cite{vaswani2017attention} with a causal mask runs over the
72 steps of each node, and its last state is the node embedding $e_n$. Rungs M0--M2 use a GRU instead.

\textbf{Regional context.} A ConvLSTM~\cite{shi2015convolutional} runs over the past grid fields, and its final hidden map
is sampled bilinearly at each node's location and concatenated to $e_n$. Fire radiative power on the grid is hidden for the
most recent hours to respect detection latency.

\textbf{Wind-aware adaptive diffusion graph.} Edges join each node to its eight nearest nodes within 600\,km, with static
weight $A_{ij}=\exp(-d_{ij}/\sigma)$ ($\sigma$ the median neighbour distance), modulated at issue time by wind alignment
\begin{equation}
\tilde A_{ij}(t_0)=A_{ij}\,\max\!\big(0,\cos(\theta^{\text{wind}}_{j}-\beta_{j\to i})\big),
\end{equation}
where $\theta^{\text{wind}}_{j}$ is the direction the past six hours of wind at sender $j$ blow toward and $\beta_{j\to i}$
is the bearing from $j$ to $i$, following PM2.5-GNN~\cite{Wang_2020}. Two residual diffusion-convolution
layers~\cite{li2017diffusion} use forward and backward random-walk transitions of $\tilde A$ and an adaptive adjacency
$\operatorname{softmax}(\operatorname{ReLU}(E_1E_2^{\top}))$ learned from node embeddings~\cite{Wu_2019}, each with diffusion
order $K=2$:
\begin{equation}
H' = H + \operatorname{ReLU}\Big(\big[H;\,\{P^{k}H\}_{P\in\mathcal{P},\,k=1..K}\big]\,W\Big).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Decoder and loss.} A two-layer MLP maps each node state to all 48 leads. In the CAMS-informed setting the latest
published CAMS forecast for each lead is added through a per-lead input. Training minimises a masked Huber loss on observed
targets only, plus the gating penalty.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\textbf{Splits (UTC).} Train 2018--2021, validation 2022, test 2023--2024 (two winters, two stubble seasons and two
Diwalis), an extra PM-only test from April to September 2025, and a prospective winter 2026--27 test from live data. The
test splits are hashed in a lock file and can only be read by one evaluation script after the model is frozen; tuning,
model selection and early stopping use validation only. A second rolling-origin fold (train to mid-2022, validation to
mid-2023, test mid-2023 to 2024) checks robustness.

\textbf{Latency rules.} At issue time $t_0$, station observations are usable up to $t_0-1$\,h, fire detections from
acquisition plus three hours, and a CAMS run $R$ only from $R+10$\,h. Past CAMS inputs therefore use short-lead forecasts
of the newest usable run rather than analyses. Normalisation statistics come from the training years. Unit tests perturb
every value after $t_0$ and check that the inputs do not change.

\textbf{Settings.} \emph{S-A} (primary) uses past data only. \emph{S-B} adds the latest published CAMS forecast at matched
leads, framing the model as CAMS post-processing. \emph{S-C} adds true future meteorology and is reported only as an upper
bound, never as a forecast.

\textbf{Baselines.} Persistence, same hour yesterday, climatology (station $\times$ month $\times$ hour), raw CAMS at
matched lead, CAMS with per-station linear bias correction, LightGBM on lags and meteorology (S-A) and as model output
statistics on CAMS (S-B), LSTM, CNN-LSTM, a ConvLSTM baseline, PM2.5-GNN with past-only meteorology~\cite{Wang_2020}, and
Graph WaveNet~\cite{Wu_2019}. All neural models share the same data loader, loss and training schedule.

\textbf{Training.} AdamW with cosine decay, batch size 32, up to 60 epochs with early stopping on validation RMSE (patience
8), mixed precision and gradient clipping. Hyperparameters are tuned on validation with at most 20 trials per rung. Final
models are trained with five seeds. Hardware: \blank{GPU, from compute.md}.

\textbf{Metrics.} RMSE, MAE, $R^2$, bias, and skill $1-\text{RMSE}/\text{RMSE}_{\text{ref}}$ against persistence and CAMS at
+1, +24 and +48\,h; breakdowns by season, Indo-Gangetic Plain versus rest, and city; hit rate, false-alarm rate and critical
success index for PM$_{2.5}>250\,\mu$g/m$^3$; AQI-category accuracy and macro-F1. Differences are tested with the
Diebold--Mariano test with the HLN correction~\cite{Diebold_1995} and a 24\,h moving-block bootstrap.

\section{Attribution Validation}
We test the gate weights with four checks, each reported as passed or failed (Table~\ref{tab:xai}).
(a) \emph{Faithfulness:} masking the top-$k$ gated groups should raise the error more than masking random groups, and
compares with the top-$k$ groups by Integrated Gradients~\cite{sundararajan2017axiomatic}.
(b) \emph{Sanity:} randomising the weights layer by layer should change the attributions~\cite{adebayo2018sanity}.
(c) \emph{Stability:} group rankings should agree across seeds.
(d) \emph{Plausibility:} for Indo-Gangetic Plain stations in October--November, the fire-group weight should rise with
upwind FIRMS counts; in March--May the dust-group weight should rise with CAMS dust optical depth; and the traffic-proxy
weight should fall during the 2020 lockdown relative to the same weeks of 2019.
We also compare group rankings with TreeSHAP on LightGBM~\cite{lundberg2017a}.

\section{Limitations}
ERA5 reanalysis stands in for operational meteorological analyses during training; the live system uses forecast
meteorology, so served skill may differ from benchmark skill. CPCB coverage after 2024 is sparse, so the 2025 check uses
